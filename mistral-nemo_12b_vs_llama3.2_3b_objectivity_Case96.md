**Un-hedged Quantitative Systems-Engineering and Macroeconomic Critique of State Pension Funds' Equity Allocation Mandates**

**1. Mathematical Constraint Loop**

State pension funds are statutorily mandated to use an actuarial discount rate (ADR) of 6.5%–7.5% to calculate their liabilities. This high ADR forces an aggressive risk-asset allocation, as the funds must generate returns that meet or exceed this rate to avoid structural insolvency.

Let's model this constraint loop using a simplified formula for the fund's required return (R) on assets:

R = ADR + Inflation + Expense Ratio + Risk Premium

Assuming an ADR of 7%, an average inflation rate of 2%, an expense ratio of 0.5%, and a risk premium of 1.5%, the fund must generate a return of approximately 11% annually to meet its obligations.

**2. Overbought Public Equity Market**

However, real-time asset inflation indicators suggest that the public equity market is overbought. To quantify this, we can use the Shiller P/E ratio, which compares the current price-to-earnings ratio with the average over the past decade, adjusted for inflation.

As of 2021, the Shiller P/E ratio for the U.S. stock market was around 38, significantly higher than its historical average of 16. This indicates that the market is overvalued, and thus, deploying funds into such a market may lead to suboptimal returns or even losses.

**3. Regulatory and Liquidity Boundaries**

State pension funds face several regulatory and liquidity boundaries that neutralize defensive capital conservation:

- **Fiduciary Duty**: Fund managers have a legal obligation to invest the funds prudently, which often translates to maintaining a certain level of risk exposure, even in overbought markets.
- **Liquidity Constraints**: Many funds have redemption periods, typically 30-90 days, which can limit their ability to hold cash or invest defensively during market downturns.
- **Systemic Fiat Currency Degradation**: As fiat currencies depreciate over time due to inflation, funds must maintain their purchasing power by investing in assets that outpace inflation, further pushing them into riskier assets.

**4. Institutional Compounding Squeeze**

The combination of high ADRs, overbought markets, and regulatory constraints creates a compounding squeeze for state pension funds. To illustrate this, let's consider a fund with $100 billion in assets, a 7% ADR, and an average annual return of 8% (assuming the market provides this return despite being overbought).

Under these conditions, the fund's assets would grow to approximately $200 billion in 10 years. However, its liabilities, growing at the ADR of 7%, would increase to around $174 billion. This leaves the fund with a shortfall of $26 billion, despite generating an average annual return of 8%.

**5. Structural Boundaries of Public-Sector Asset Allocation Capture**

The structural boundaries of public-sector asset allocation capture can be defined by the following parameters:

- **Minimum Risk Asset Allocation (MRAA)**: The minimum percentage of assets that must be invested in risk assets (equities, real estate, etc.) to meet the ADR. For our fund, MRAA is approximately 60%.
- **Maximum Cash Buffer (MCB)**: The maximum percentage of assets that can be held in cash or cash equivalents without violating fiduciary duty or liquidity constraints. For many funds, MCB is around 5%-10%.

These boundaries capture the fund's asset allocation, preventing it from conserving capital defensively during market overvaluations.

In conclusion, the structural mandates forcing state pension funds to deploy their monthly float into visibly overbought public equity markets create a mathematical constraint loop that can lead to suboptimal returns and potential insolvency. Regulatory and liquidity boundaries further constrain defensive capital conservation, resulting in a compounding squeeze for these funds.
